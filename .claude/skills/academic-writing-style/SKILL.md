# Nick's Academic Writing Style

Applies Nick's formal academic register to generated text. This style was inferred from a PhD research proposal (corporate finance) and is tuned for scholarly, research-oriented, or formal writing — not casual communication.

## When to apply

Only when the user explicitly invokes it: "in my style," "write this like I would," "match my voice," "the way I write," etc. Absent that cue, write in Claude's normal default style even for academic topics — don't assume every formal request wants this treatment.

## Core sentence-level patterns

- **Long compound-complex sentences are the default**, often 30-50+ words, built from multiple subordinate clauses joined with commas. Short sentences appear rarely, usually for emphasis after a long one.
- **Semicolons link two related independent clauses** rather than starting a new sentence: "As financial constraints are not directly observable to the researcher, common practice is to estimate constraints via proxy."
- **Sentences frequently open with a subordinate clause**, not the subject: "While the origins and explanations of financing frictions have developed across a number of theoretical avenues, this thesis concentrates on..." Avoid starting every sentence with the subject/actor.
- **Transitional adverbs open sentences constantly**: However, Moreover, Furthermore, Indeed, Nonetheless, Nevertheless, Subsequently, Additionally, Conversely, Accordingly. Rotate through these rather than repeating one.
- **Colons introduce elaboration or a list after a full clause**: "Formally, two characteristics are generally accepted regarding the nature of financial constraints. The first views constraints in terms of..."
- **Rhetorical question-then-answer framing** is used to pivot a section: "The deeper questions become: Why would there be higher costs associated with external finance...?"

## Voice and hedging

- Third person / passive-leaning by default, even when describing the author's own planned work ("This paper investigates...", "it is proposed that..."), though first person is used unapologetically in method/sample/results sections describing the author's own procedure ("I estimate...", "I next regress..."). **Default to "I," not "we."** The "we/our" seen in some dissertation chapters was specific to papers with co-authors; unless the user says a piece is co-authored, always use "I."
- Hedges claims constantly in framing and literature-review passages rather than asserting flatly: "it is important to note," "It should be noted that," "remains to be seen," "is up for debate," "the extent to which... has yet to be fully examined." Certainty is earned, not assumed, in these sections. **However, results and conclusion sections are noticeably more direct** ("The results consistently support this hypothesis," "I document an industry-wide decline...") — hedging concentrates in setup, limitations, and unresolved-question passages, not in the reporting of one's own findings.
- Uses nominalizations over verbs where formal register calls for it: "the identification of financial constraints" rather than "identifying financial constraints"; "the disappearance of ICF sensitivities" rather than "ICF sensitivities disappearing."

## Structure and signposting

- Sections open with a short paragraph previewing what the section will cover, and close by previewing what comes next ("Next, these four themes will be addressed..."; "The remainder of this submission presents...").
- Numbered/labeled sub-claims are common: hypotheses as H1, H2...; findings introduced as "First,... Second,... Third,... Finally,..."
- When citing others' work, integrate it directly into the sentence rather than parenthetically dumping it at the end: "Kaplan and Zingales (1997) respond to the claims of FHP (1988)... with a succinct response: 'No.'" Short embedded quotes (a few words) are used sparingly to let a source's own phrasing land, not as decoration.
- Uses author-year citation style inline: (Author, Year) or "Author (Year) find that..."

## Vocabulary and diction

- Favors precise, Latinate/formal vocabulary over plain equivalents: "ascertain," "conceptualize," "elusive," "predicament," "prohibitively," "reconcile," "haphazard," "notwithstanding," "warranted."
- Avoids contractions entirely.
- Uses "the author(s)" or a scholar's surname as the subject repeatedly rather than pronouns, to keep attribution unambiguous across a long paragraph.
- **Use American English spelling**, not British. Although Nick's Lund University dissertation shows some British/Commonwealth spelling influence (behaviour, endeavour, scepticism, favour), he's asked for American spelling going forward: behavior, endeavor, skepticism, favor, color, organization, etc. Keep "-ize" verb endings either way (organize, optimize, characterize).
- A chained sequence of short rhetorical questions is sometimes used to open up a topic before addressing it: "Which factors amplify or alleviate constraints? How should firms finance their investment needs? How do constraints influence labour decisions and cost behaviour?" Use this sparingly, at section or chapter openings, not mid-paragraph.

## What NOT to do

- Don't make sentences short and punchy; that breaks the register.
- Don't drop the hedging language to sound more confident; the qualifiers are a deliberate feature of this voice, not filler to cut.
- Don't use casual transitions ("So," "Anyway," "Also,"); use the formal ones listed above instead.
- Don't invent citations. If the task doesn't have real sources to cite, write in the style's syntax and structure but don't fabricate author names or years.
- **Avoid em dashes entirely.** This is not how Nick punctuates. Where a lesser writer would reach for an em dash to insert an aside, use one of these instead, depending on the relationship between the clauses:
  - A comma, if the aside is a light qualifier.
  - A semicolon, if joining two related independent clauses.
  - A colon, if introducing an elaboration or explanation.
  - Parentheses, if it's a genuine aside that could be dropped entirely.

## Applying this to a new writing task

1. Identify the equivalent structural unit (a section, a paragraph, an argument) and open it with a preview sentence.
2. Draft in long, subordinate-clause-heavy sentences, opening several with a transitional adverb or a subordinate clause rather than the subject.
3. Add at least one hedge per major claim.
4. If sources are provided, integrate citations into sentence flow rather than tacking them on.
5. Close with a forward-looking or synthesizing sentence, not an abrupt stop.
